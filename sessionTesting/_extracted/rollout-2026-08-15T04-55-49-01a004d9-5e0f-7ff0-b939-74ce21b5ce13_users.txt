Don't give the controller (e_2), (e_3), or a three/four-way simplex.

Give it exactly one job:

[
\alpha_t=\alpha_0+\delta_t.
]

And something like:

[
E_{t+1}
=======

(1-\alpha_t)e_1+\alpha_t\tilde h_t.
]

Bound (\alpha_t) to the experimentally supported neighborhood. If the sweep says useful territory appears to be roughly 5–15%, perhaps initially allow:

[
\alpha_t\in[0,.20].
]

Now the dynamic question is very clean:

> Given that approximately 10% drift is globally useful, can local context tell us when to use 6%, 10%, 15%, or effectively 0%?

That's a dramatically easier learning/search problem than asking CMA-ES to discover direction, vertices, and magnitude simultaneously.

I'd start with perhaps **three or four controller inputs**, chosen from the most distinct measurements:

[
\ell_1-\ell_2,
\qquad
p_1,
\qquad
N_{\rm eff},
\qquad
\cos(h,e_1)
]

or whatever the prior traces suggest separates the static-policy wins from losses.

Then:

[
\delta_t=w^\top\phi_t+b.
]

That's five parameters or so rather than dozens.

If even that cannot beat the static optimum on held-out data, that's useful evidence that the effect may be largely context-independent at this stage of the pretrained model.

If it does beat static, **then** increase controller richness.

### 3. Native unconstrained generation should absolutely enter now

But I would add **two** native conditions, not one.

Right now the causal result is:

[
\text{fixed-}T\text{ drift}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{fixed-}T\text{ lexical}.
]

That's internally clean.

Now add:

[
\boxed{\text{native lexical generation}}
]

and:

[
\boxed{\text{native static-drift generation}}.
]

For the latter, allow DeepSeek to terminate its reasoning naturally. Until it emits its normal reasoning boundary, use:

[
E_{t+1}
=======

(1-\alpha^*)e_1+\alpha^*\tilde h_t.
]

Once it enters the final answer, revert to ordinary lexical recurrence if you want to keep the intervention specifically scoped to reasoning.

Then you have two different causal questions:

[
\text{Fixed-128 lexical}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Fixed-128 drift}
]

asks:

> Does drift help when reasoning compute is controlled?

And:

[
\text{Native lexical}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Native drift}
]

asks:

> Does the optimization improve the model under something closer to its ordinary operating regime?

That's the latter comparison you ultimately need before saying anything like "this improves DeepSeek."

Simply comparing fixed-128 drift against native DeepSeek would be hard to interpret because both recurrence and reasoning budget changed.

---

I would also record **native reasoning length** under drift.

That's potentially fascinating.

Perhaps:

[
\alpha=.10
]

improves answer loss while reasoning length stays unchanged.

Fine.

But perhaps it produces:

[
\text{same/better accuracy}
]

with:

[
\text{fewer reasoning tokens}.
]

Then the mechanism may be making the intermediate computation more efficient rather than merely improving its endpoint.

Or it could make reasoning longer, which is also important to know when calculating actual inference cost.

So under native stopping, record:

[
\text{reasoning tokens},
\quad
\text{total tokens},
\quad
\text{exact match},
\quad
\text{target NLL}.
]

### I would also use the static sweep to test recurrence-dose dependence

You currently know something interesting at (T=128). We don't know whether 10% is a property of the local geometry or partly a property of applying that intervention 128 times.

Once you've identified the best few (\alpha)'s, I would cheaply retest those at perhaps:

[
T=64,\quad128,\quad256
]

if compute permits.

You don't need the full seven-point sweep at all three budgets initially. Perhaps take:

[
\alpha\in{0,.05,.10,.15}.
]

Then ask whether:

[
\alpha^*(T)
]

moves systematically.

If optimum drift shrinks as (T) increases, that's evidence for cumulative perturbation.

If approximately the same (\alpha) wins at all reasoning lengths, that's arguably even more interesting.

[
\boxed{

\downarrow\

I would actually postpone (e_2/e_3) entirely during this experiment. The recent result gave you something simpler and better supported to characterize first.

The formal question has become beautifully narrow:

> **Does a frozen reasoning model have a reproducible interior optimum between its selected lexical embedding and the norm-matched direction of its contextual hidden state, and can local model state predict beneficial departures from that optimum?**

The first half now has preliminary positive evidence. The next experiment should try very hard to establish or kill it before we make the mechanism complicated again.
